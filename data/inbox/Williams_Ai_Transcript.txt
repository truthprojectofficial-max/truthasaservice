What we're saying is that when this Court specifically interprets a federal statute to prohibit certain requirements, states cannot resurrect those requirements through labeling. Alabama is trying to circumvent those holdings by calling its exhaustion requirement "jurisdictional." If we allow that, then every state could nullify federal rights through creative labeling. That's not weakening state jurisdictional authority—that's enforcing the Supremacy Clause when states try to defeat specific federal guarantees this Court has already recognized. JUSTICE JACKSON: And are you saying that, assuming

ROBOT ADAM: Justice Jackson, state jurisdictional rules can absolutely be preempted. That's what Felder shows—Wisconsin could have called its notice requirement "jurisdictional," but it would still be preempted because it conflicts with federal law. The key is that preemption analysis looks at what the rule does, not what it's called. If a state jurisdictional rule conflicts with federal law—by defeating federal rights or creating non-uniform enforcement—it's preempted under the Supremacy Clause. Here, even assuming Alabama's rule is jurisdictional, it still requires exhaustion of administrative remedies before accessing any court. JUSTICE KAVANAUGH: Well, what is the real-world problem with just going to federal court? Or maybe there aren't any and you're just saying precedent says we can go to state court. JUSTICE KAVANAUGH: And you mentioned this earlier, but a procedural due process claim usually requires that you go through the state processes first. ROBOT ADAM: Mr. Chief Justice, the Kafkaesque element is that Alabama uses its own violations to prevent judicial review of those violations. You can't challenge inadequate notice because the inadequate notice prevented you from completing the process. JUSTICE GORSUCH: This new narrow theory, what -- what exactly does it encompass? What class of cases?
